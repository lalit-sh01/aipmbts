My design partner changes direction every week

Most of these changes fall into five kinds of request, and each kind needs a different response from you.

Design partner here means anyone you co-build an early AI product with: an external customer, a client of your IT services firm, or a business unit inside your own company. Where the advice differs by setting, a note says so.

The challenge

What is happening. You are building an AI contract tool with the procurement team of a large manufacturer. In week 1 they asked for clause extraction from supplier contracts. In week 3 they wanted risk scoring instead. In week 5, after their CFO saw a competitor's demo, the ask became Tamil summaries for plant managers.

Why it hurts. Your team has built three different versions in 5 weeks, so nothing is finished or properly tested. Asking for a change costs the partner nothing and costs you weeks of work. The plant managers it is meant for have barely used it. The pilot ends in 6 weeks with no agreed definition of success, and your leadership has already presented it as a flagship AI win, so stepping back now looks like failure.

What is really going on

It is tempting to blame an indecisive partner. Look at the requests one by one, though, and five different kinds of request appear. Each needs a different response.

01

A complaint in disguise

They say"Can we add a screen where my team can correct the extracted clauses?"

What is really happening
The current output is getting things wrong. Asking for a new feature feels politer than saying so.
How to spot it
Ask what prompted the request. They will show you an output your product got wrong.
What to do
Fix the existing feature first. A new feature built on top carries the same errors.
02

Their standard is sharpening

They say"Can the summaries be shorter and more formal?"

What is really happening
They are working out what good looks like by reading your outputs. One study found people form their standard this way [1].
How to spot it
They point to a specific output they did not like.
What to do
Add those outputs and their corrected versions to a working test set, a list of real examples with agreed good answers. Your team then measures against their new standard.
03

Someone else's idea

They say"Our CFO wants Tamil summaries for plant managers."

What is really happening
A senior person outside the day-to-day work saw a competitor's demo and wants the same thing, whether or not it solves this team's problem.
How to spot it
They point to a meeting or a demo, not to a problem in their own work.
What to do
Ask two plant managers whether the English summaries are hard for them to use today. If they are, treat it as a new problem. If not, show the budget owner what the plant managers said, and keep it out of the pilot unless it gets its own time and budget.
04

A genuinely new problem

They say"Our buyers also want an alert before a supplier contract renews."

What is really happening
Your product has exposed a real problem nobody had noticed before.
How to spot it
They point to a step in their daily work.
What to do
Note it. Put it on the roadmap when a second partner or business unit asks for the same thing, or when the budget owner funds it on its own.
05

Curiosity, not need

They say"Could it also do something with our emails?"

What is really happening
They are excited about AI but do not feel the problem strongly enough to commit time or data.
How to spot it
Nobody can point to an output, a workflow or a person who needs it.
What to do
Ask what they would commit to test it: time, data or budget. If the answer is nothing, see the lighter-role option below.
Stop asking

What do you want?

Start asking

What did you see that made you want this?

The mistake most teams make

Trying to stop the changes by freezing the feature list in a signed requirements document.

The feature list was written before anyone had judged a real output, so it locks in a standard the partner has already outgrown. The person who signs it often does not control the budget. The partner learns that feedback is pointless, goes quiet, and the pilot ends politely without a decision. Sign off the problem and the fixed test set instead, and let the features change.

The options you have

Most teams combine two or three of these. Pick the ones your team's capacity and the partner's commitment can support.

Widen to more partners

Bring in 2 or 3 more partners with the same problem, and hold off on big build decisions until you can compare what they each ask for.

Best when
One partner is driving most of your backlog.
What it costs you
Several weeks of finding and onboarding partners, during which you build very little that is new.
Watch out
It fails if the new partners have a different problem, because their requests will pull the product apart instead of confirming it.

Inside a company or a services client, widen by adding a second business unit or a second team with the same problem.

Ask for a real commitment

Agree what the partner puts in before you build more. For a customer, that can be a fee. The first go-to-market hire at Sierra, a US customer-service AI company, recommends 10–20% of contract value, because anything less is not a real commitment [2].

Best when
The partner feels the problem strongly and someone on their side controls a budget.
What it costs you
Some partners will walk away. In India, founders and investors describe repeated requests for free proofs of concept [3], so many partners will not pay a fee at this stage. Ask for data, named expert time and a signed decision date instead, and treat a refusal of all three as a no.
Watch out
It fails if the ask stops with your champion and never reaches the person who controls the budget.

For an internal team, the commitment is a budget line, named people's time and data access. For a services firm, it is a paid proof of concept written into the statement of work.

Test new ideas with a quick prototype first

When a new request arrives, build a rough version in a few days on the partner's real examples before committing your engineering team. For example, run 20 real contracts through a rough renewal alert and show the results to two buyers.

Best when
Requests keep changing and each one costs you weeks of rebuilding.
What it costs you
A few days of one person's time per idea, and the discipline to throw most prototypes away.
Watch out
A prototype that looks finished raises expectations. Label it clearly as a test, and agree beforehand what result would earn it a place on the roadmap.

Embed an engineer with the partner

Place one of your engineers with the partner's team for part of each week. Anything that two or more partners ask for gets built into the core product.

Best when
The work is valuable and similar requests keep coming from several partners.
What it costs you
One engineer's time goes to one partner, and the partner may start treating that engineer as their own staff.
Watch out
It turns into a consulting arrangement when most requests are one-off workflows that will never become part of the product.

Scale back to a lighter role

Pause building for this partner. Keep them informed monthly and put your team's time where there is real commitment.

Best when
The partner will not commit time, data or budget, and the problem does not seem to hurt them much.
What it costs you
The executive who championed the pilot may feel dropped, and a partnership your leadership has showcased goes quiet. Agree the move with your own leadership first.
Watch out
It fails if this partner is your only way into a market or business area that matters to your strategy.

PM tools to put in place

Examples with every request

Each new request comes with about 10 real examples from the partner's work and what a good result looks like, approved by one named person. Ideas that started in someone else's demo rarely come with examples, so they filter themselves out.

A request log

One row per request: who asked, what they had just seen, and the examples. The last column says which kind of request it is: a complaint in disguise, a sharpening standard, someone else's idea, a new problem, or curiosity.

Two test sets

Keep a fixed set of about 100 real examples with agreed good answers. It does not change during the pilot and decides the go/no-go. Keep a working set too, where new examples of good are added each week.

As of September 2026, if the partner is an Indian bank or a larger NBFC, RBI's outsourcing rules, updated in November 2025, require due diligence and regular audits of IT vendors like you [4][5]. For any Indian partner, the DPDP Rules, notified in November 2025, phase in data-protection duties over 18 months [6], so real examples that contain personal data need masking. Plan for both test sets to be built on masked data inside the partner's systems, and allow weeks for data-access approval.

A signed-off problem, not a feature list

Sign off the problem to solve, the fixed test set, the one person who judges good, and the decision date. Features can change any week. A change to the problem itself is decided with the budget owner at your regular review. If the problem changes, agree a new fixed set at the same review.

Your first three steps

  1. 01

    Put every request since the pilot began into the request log and mark what kind each one is. The mix shows whether you mainly have a quality problem, a problem of who decides and commits, or a real new need.

  2. 02

    Agree with the partner that new requests will come with examples from their work. Present it as the way to make sure you build what their team actually needs.

  3. 03

    Get 30 minutes with the budget owner this week to agree who judges good, the decision date, and what they will commit if the pilot works. With 6 weeks left, anything not agreed now gets decided by default.

What to measure, and how

Look at these every week, in this order. The first matters most.

01

Are the people who do the work actually using it?

When the people doing the work use it every week, their needs shape the product and fewer requests arrive from people who do not use it. In a report published in August 2026, enterprise buyers ranked end-user adoption as the top factor in deciding whether to scale an AI pilot [7], so expect your budget owner to judge yours on use too.

How to measure it
Count the real pieces of work run through the tool each week, such as contracts reviewed, and how many of its outputs were used without being rewritten.
Good sign
Both numbers grow week on week, and users ask for improvements to what already exists.
Warning sign
Use is flat or falling, and most requests come from people who do not use the product.
What to do: Spend a day with the users to find what is stopping them before you build anything new.
02

Is the partner committing more over time?

A partner who gives a little more each month is moving towards a decision to scale. A partner who gives nothing new is not.

  1. Shows interest
  2. Shares real data
  3. Gives expert time
  4. Signs a decision date
  5. Commits budget
How to measure it
At each review with the budget owner, mark which step of the ladder they have reached. In a short pilot, review every two weeks so you get more than one reading.
Good sign
They move up at least one step between reviews.
Warning sign
They stay on the same step for two reviews in a row, or ask to extend the pilot without committing anything new.
What to do: Ask the budget owner for the next step by a set date.
03

Is it getting better at what they already asked for?

While the current job stays unreliable, new requests keep arriving to work around it, so fixing it cuts requests at the source.

How to measure it
Run both test sets after every change. A rise on the fixed set counts only if the working set rises too; otherwise the team is tuning to the test.
Good sign
Both pass rates rise, and nobody has changed the fixed set.
Warning sign
The fixed set's pass rate is flat or rises on its own, or someone asks to change the fixed set.
What to do: Pause new features and fix quality on the current use case.
04

Is the rebuilding under control?

Rebuilding is the real cost of shifting direction, and it is a number your own leadership will immediately understand.

How to measure it
Each week, tag your team's work as either new work or redoing something already built, and track the share spent redoing.
Good sign
The share falls steadily after the first month.
Warning sign
It stays high, or jumps after a senior person sees a demo.
What to do: Show the number to the budget owner and agree which requests to stop.

Numbers that look good but mislead

  • Requests shipped. A team can ship many changes while quality and use stand still.
  • Meetings held. Frequent meetings cost the partner nothing, so they say little about commitment.
  • Praise after a demo. A champion's enthusiasm does not tell you whether the budget owner will commit.

Real examples

NamedFirst-hand account

Sierra

The company set out to land 4 design partners from a list of 40 prospects, choosing those that felt the problem most. It ended with 6, and all 6 became customers. Its first go-to-market hire traced the one early partner that "kept changing what they wanted" to a partner that "didn't feel the pain as acutely" [2].

The lessonScreen for how much the problem hurts before the partnership starts. It prevents more direction changes than any process you add later.

Identity concealedResearch interview

A buyer in MIT's 2025 study

One of the buyers interviewed for MIT NANDA's 2025 study said, "ChatGPT consistently produces better outputs, even though our vendor claims to use the same underlying technology" [8].

The lessonRun a sample of the partner's own examples through the chatbot their staff already use, and show both results side by side. With a bank or other regulated partner, mask customer data first or use the chatbot the firm has approved.

NamedSecondary report

Axis Bank

Prasad Lad, who heads Axis Bank's Business Intelligence Unit, argued that enterprises should not write off AI proofs of concept, saying "there is no path to enterprise scaling till you have done the POC right" [9].

The lessonWith a bank or other regulated partner, present the pilot as the first step to production and agree that path at the start, rather than treating it as an experiment.
