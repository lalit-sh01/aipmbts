My design partner changes direction every week

Most of these changes fall into five kinds of request, and each kind needs a different response from you.

A design partner could be a customer, a services client, or a team inside your own company.

The challenge

You’re building an AI contract tool with a manufacturer’s procurement team. In week 1 they wanted clause extraction. By week 3 it was risk scoring. Then their CFO saw a competitor’s demo, and now it’s Tamil summaries for plant managers.

Nothing is finished, and the plant managers it’s meant for have barely used it. The pilot ends in 6 weeks, nobody has agreed what success looks like, and your leadership has already presented it as a flagship AI win. Stepping back now would look like failure.

What is really going on

So your partner keeps changing what they want. Every week there’s something new, and your team is rebuilding again. It’s frustrating, and it’s easy to think they just can’t make up their mind.

But if you go back and look at each request on its own, they’re usually not the same kind of request at all. Some are about quality, some came from someone else, and some are just curiosity. Each kind needs a different answer from you.

The simplest way to tell them apart? Next time a request comes in, ask what made them ask for it. Whatever they point to tells you a lot.

If they show you an output you got wrong, it’s really a complaint. “Can we add a screen where my team can correct the extracted clauses?” is them telling you, politely, that the extraction isn’t good enough yet. Fix that first, because anything you build on top will carry the same errors.

If they show you an output they didn’t like, their bar is going up. “Can the summaries be shorter and more formal?” means they’re figuring out what good looks like by reading what you produce [1]. That’s a good sign, honestly. Save those examples so your team tests against what they want now.

If they mention a meeting or a demo, it’s probably someone else’s idea. “Our CFO wants Tamil summaries” came from a competitor’s demo, not from the plant managers. Before anyone builds it, ask two plant managers whether the English summaries actually give them trouble.

If they point to something in their daily work, you may have found a real new problem. “Our buyers want an alert before a supplier contract renews” is that kind. Write it down. It’s worth building when a second team asks for it too, or when someone is ready to fund it.

And if they can’t point to anything, it’s mostly curiosity. “Could it also do something with our emails?” is excitement about AI more than a real need. Ask what they’d put in to try it: some time, some data, a bit of budget. If the answer is nothing, it can wait.

The mistake most teams make

The natural move is to freeze the feature list and get it signed. But nobody had judged a real output when that list was written, so they’ve outgrown it within weeks. The partner learns feedback doesn’t change anything, goes quiet, and the pilot just fizzles out.

Get the problem signed off instead, along with a fixed test set, and let the features keep changing.

The options you have

Most teams pick two or three of these.

If you’re asking a customer for a commitment, it can be a fee, and Sierra’s first go-to-market hire suggests 10–20% of contract value [2]. In India, founders and investors describe repeated requests for free proofs of concept [3], so ask for data, named expert time and a signed decision date instead. If they say no to all three, that’s your answer.

Inside your company, or for a services clientWiden by bringing in a second team with the same problem. The commitment is a budget line, named people’s time and data access, or for a services firm, a paid proof of concept in the statement of work.

PM tools to put in place

Keep a request log. Each request gets a row with what they’d just seen, about 10 real examples, and which kind it is. Ideas from someone else’s demo rarely come with examples, so they filter themselves out.

You’ll also want two test sets. The fixed one, about 100 examples, stays locked and decides go or no-go. The working one grows every week with what they want now.

And get one page signed off: the problem, the fixed test set, who judges good, and the decision date.

Your first three steps

  1. Put every request so far into the log and mark its kind. Once you see them together, you’ll know whether you’re mainly dealing with quality, with who decides and commits, or with a real new need.
  2. Ask the partner to send examples with every new request, so you build what their team actually needs.
  3. Get 30 minutes with the budget owner this week to agree who judges good, the decision date, and what they’ll commit if it works. With 6 weeks left, anything you don’t agree now gets decided by default.

What to measure, and how

Look at these every week, in this order. The first one matters most.

01

Are the people who do the work actually using it?

Count the contracts that go through it each week and how many outputs are used without rewriting. Buyers rank this above everything else when deciding to scale [4]. If it’s flat, spend a day with the users before building anything new.

02

Is the partner committing more over time?

At every review, mark which step they’re on. If they’re stuck on the same step for two reviews, ask the budget owner for the next one by a set date.

03

Is it getting better at what they already asked for?

If it still isn’t reliable at the job it already has, they’ll keep sending new requests to work around it. Run both test sets after every change. If the fixed set goes up, it only counts when the working set goes up too. Otherwise your team is tuning to the test.

If you see the right-hand picture, or someone asks to change the fixed set, pause new features and fix quality on what they already asked for.

04

Is the rebuilding under control?

Each week, track how much of your team’s time goes into redoing things. It should fall after the first month. If it jumps after a senior person sees a demo, show that number to the budget owner and agree which requests to stop.

Numbers that look good but misleadRequests shipped, because you can ship more and more while quality and use stay where they are. Meetings held, because they cost the partner nothing. Praise after a demo, because it shows your champion is excited, but not whether the budget owner will commit.

Real examples

Named · first-hand account

Sierra

Sierra builds AI agents for customer service. In 2023 their first go-to-market hire, Logan Randolph, set out to find their first design partners, and two of the partners he signed tell this whole lesson.

The first one kept changing what they wanted. Looking back, Logan put it down to how they’d picked that partner more than to the requests themselves. This partner didn’t feel the pain the way the others did. They wanted to use AI agents and went looking for a problem to put one on.

SiriusXM pushed just as hard, but for something specific. They wanted voice. SiriusXM became the voice design partner and launched first. They also wanted a proper way to give feedback on their agent, instead of the CSV exports Sierra was sending. That push is where Agent Studio came from [2].

Both partners asked for a lot. What made the difference was why they were asking. SiriusXM’s requests gave Sierra a product, and the first partner’s just kept moving.

That’s what Logan screened for. He looked for partners with real pain, at real scale. His advice is to charge partners 10–20% of the contract value. A boss has to sign that off, and it filters out teams that are only curious about AI [2].

The lessonWhen a partner keeps changing what they want, ask what made them ask. If they have a real problem, their requests make your product better. If they’re just looking for somewhere to use AI, the target keeps moving.
